Where this proposal starts

The software is the smaller half of the problem.

Anyone can build a screen that accepts £500. The hard part is what happens in the ninety seconds afterwards: the funds are collected under one regulator, converted at a rate you quoted before you owned the currency, screened against sanctions lists in two jurisdictions, paid into an Indian account under FEMA, and reconciled the next morning against three separate statements that will not agree.

What we are building

A transaction orchestration platform: customer apps, a double-entry transfer engine, a compliance and operations console, and a reconciliation layer that sits on top of licensed financial institutions in both countries.

What we are not building

A bank. We do not build a wallet, an FX book, a correspondent network or a payout network. Those are bought from regulated providers, because building them multiplies cost and time by an order of magnitude.

What decides the price

Three answers: who holds customer money, which Indian inward-remittance channel you use, and how many pay-in methods you support at launch. Phase 0 fixes all three before a line of production code is written.

One correction we want to make early, because it changes the architecture

Preliminary studies of this project have referenced RBI's Payment Aggregator – Cross Border (PA-CB) framework. PA-CB governs online commercial payments for goods and services. Personal family remittance into India travels a different road: either the Money Transfer Service Scheme (MTSS) or a Rupee Drawing Arrangement (RDA) with an AD Category-I bank. These are not interchangeable, and the choice imposes hard product limits — see the regulatory section. Designing against the wrong framework is the single most expensive mistake available on this project.

    Why this corridor

    The route is proven. The margin is contested.

    This is not a market you need to create. It is a market you need to enter with lower friction than nine incumbents who are already very good at it.

    £3.3bn+Flowed from the UK to India in 2024 — over a third of all UK outbound remittance value
    2m+Individual UK→India transfers a year; the second-largest UK corridor by volume
    $135bnIndia's total remittance receipts in FY25, up roughly 14% year on year
    3.55%Average all-in cost at digital-first operators, against 14.55% at banks

    What this means commercially

    The UK→India corridor is already one of the cheapest in the world — roughly 1.75%–3.2% all-in depending on ticket size, against a 6.36% global average. There is no room to win on being marginally cheaper than Wise. The defensible positions are speed to a specific payout rail, first-transfer conversion, recipient-side experience, and serving a segment the incumbents treat generically: students, seasonal workers, NRI property and education payers, or a named regional community.

    We recommend the platform be built to support segment-specific journeys from day one — configurable limits, purpose codes and messaging per segment — rather than a single generic flow that has to be retrofitted later.

    What this means technically

    Thin margins make cost-per-transaction an architectural constraint, not an accounting detail. Three consequences run through this entire proposal:

    • Pay-in must default to account-to-account, with cards as an exception path only
    • KYC spend must be amortised — verify once, reuse across the customer lifetime, and never re-run a full check where a refresh will do
    • Manual operations must be designed out. Every hour of human review is a real cost per transfer; the compliance console exists to make review rare and fast

    The part that is not negotiable

    Two regulators, one product.

    Every screen in this application is shaped by a rule written in London or Mumbai. Below is the map we design against — and the specific places where regulation stops being paperwork and becomes a product decision.

    United Kingdom · FCA

    Collecting pounds is a regulated activity

    Money remittance is a payment service under the Payment Services Regulations 2017. The operating question is whether you perform it under your own permission or as an agent of a firm that holds one.

    UK permission routes
    Route Threshold & capital Gateway
    Agent of an authorised PI
    Fastest to market; the principal owns the permission and the risk    		 No own capital; principal's due diligence and agent registration 4–10 wks
    Small Payment Institution
    Lighter registration; safeguarding not legally mandated    		 Average monthly turnover under €3m 3–6 mo
    Authorised Payment Institution
    Full permission; assessed on governance, capital, safeguarding, AML, business model, systems    		 £20,000 initial capital for money remittance 6–12 mo

    Three UK rules that write themselves into the code

    • Safeguarding, strengthened from 7 May 2026. Under PS25/12, in-scope firms must keep prescribed books and records, maintain a resolution pack, submit monthly safeguarding returns and obtain acknowledgement letters in prescribed form. This is a reporting system we build, not a policy document you file.
    • APP fraud reimbursement. Since 7 October 2024 sending firms must reimburse eligible scam victims within five business days, up to £85,000 per claim, with a £100 excess and a 13-month claim window, and cost split 50/50 with the receiving firm. That liability is a direct input to our fraud engine, warning screens and Confirmation of Payee flow.
    • Strong customer authentication and Consumer Duty. SCA under reg. 100 governs the login and payment journey; Consumer Duty governs how clearly the rate, the fee and the delivery time are presented before the customer commits.

    Authorisation is also harder than it was: FCA refusal rates for payment institution applications have risen from roughly 1 in 14 in 2021 to about 1 in 5. We plan for a rejected first attempt as a live scenario, not an unthinkable one.

    India · RBI & FEMA

    Paying out rupees is a different regime entirely

    Personal inward remittance runs on two RBI channels. They are not equivalent, and the choice constrains what the app is allowed to let a customer do.

    Indian inward channels
    Channel Scope Hard limits
    MTSS
    Overseas Principal ties up with an authorised Indian Agent    		 Personal remittance only. No trade, no property purchase, no investment, no NRE/FCNR credit, no charitable donation USD 2,500 per transaction; 30 remittances per beneficiary per year; cash payout capped at ₹50,000
    RDA
    AD Category-I bank with an approved overseas exchange house    		 Personal remittance with no cap; trade remittance capped per transaction No cap on personal transfers; no cash payout; trade use restricted

    Why this is a product decision, not a legal footnote

    If you launch on MTSS, the app must enforce a per-transaction ceiling of roughly £2,000 and a 30-transfer annual counter per beneficiary — which means the platform needs beneficiary-level cumulative tracking, a counter visible to the customer, and graceful handling of the transfer that would breach it. If you launch on RDA, those ceilings disappear but cash payout does, and your partner set changes completely. The transfer engine we build supports both as configuration, but the launch channel must be fixed in Phase 0 because it determines the partner shortlist.

    Payout rails

    IMPS and NEFT to bank accounts are the reliable base. UPI payout for inward remittance is real but partner-dependent — it is delivered through an Indian bank connected to NPCI on behalf of money transfer operators, not by integrating UPI directly. NPCI's own international build-out is a country-by-country programme; treat UPI payout as a partner capability to be verified in Phase 0, never as an assumption.

    Data and privacy

    RBI's payment data storage direction requires end-to-end payment transaction data to be stored on systems in India. The UK side sits under UK GDPR, and Indian personal data under the DPDP Act 2023. This is why the architecture is dual-region by design and not a single AWS account in one continent — retrofitting data residency after launch is a rebuild.

    Also in scope: FEMA purpose codes captured at the point of transfer, PAN capture thresholds, beneficiary name matching, and FIRC-equivalent documentation where the recipient requires proof of source.

    Our position

    VAW Technologies is a technology partner, not a law firm. We do not provide regulatory advice and we do not warrant licensing outcomes. What we do is build to a regulatory specification, and we insist that specification is written down and signed off by your appointed UK and Indian counsel in Phase 0. We will run those sessions, produce the traceability matrix that maps every rule to a system control, and hold ourselves to it in testing. Named counsel on both sides is a client-side dependency and a condition of the Phase 1 start.

    How it is built

    A ledger with an app attached.

    Most remittance startups fail at reconciliation, not at the user interface. So we invert the usual order and build the money-truth layer first.

    The transfer engine

    A transfer is a long-running, distributed, partially-failing process across systems that do not share a transaction boundary. We treat it as such.

    • Immutable double-entry ledger. Every movement posts balanced entries. Balances are derived, never edited. No process can update a row that represents money.
    • Explicit state machine. Every transfer has one legal state and an enumerated set of transitions. Undefined states cannot be reached, which is what makes support answerable.
    • Saga orchestration with compensations. Collected pounds but the payout partner rejected? A defined compensating path returns funds and posts the reversal. No silent losses.
    • Idempotency everywhere. Every command carries a key. Duplicate submissions, retried webhooks and mobile network flakes cannot create a second payout.
    • Transactional outbox. Events reach the queue if and only if the database commit succeeded. This closes the most common source of phantom transfers.
    • Reconciliation as a first-class service. Daily three-way match with an exception queue, break ageing and a required disposition for every unmatched item.

    The life of a transfer

    1. 01 · QuoteRate pulled from the FX provider, margin applied, quote persisted with an expiry. The quote is a record, not a UI value.
    2. 02 · InstructCustomer commits. Limits, channel caps, beneficiary counters and sanctions status are checked synchronously. Transfer created; ledger opens the obligation.
    3. 03 · CollectOpen banking payment initiated or card charged. Funds confirmed into the safeguarded or partner-held account. Nothing moves onward until settlement is confirmed, not merely authorised.
    4. 04 · ScreenSanctions, PEP and adverse-media checks on sender and beneficiary; rules engine scores the transaction. Clean transfers pass through in milliseconds; the rest enter the review queue with a service-level clock.
    5. 05 · ConvertFX executed with the partner against the locked quote. Any slippage between quoted and executed rate posts to a margin account — visible, measured, managed. It does not silently become someone's loss.
    6. 06 · Pay outPayout instruction to the Indian partner with purpose code and beneficiary detail. IMPS, NEFT or UPI depending on amount, hour and partner capability.
    7. 07 · ConfirmPartner callback or polled status resolves the transfer. Customer and recipient both notified. Receipt generated and stored.
    8. 08 · ReconcileNext-cycle three-way match. Every break is aged, owned and dispositioned. This is the step that makes an audit survivable.
